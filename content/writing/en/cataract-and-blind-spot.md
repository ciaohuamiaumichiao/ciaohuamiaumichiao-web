That day I went to an eye clinic on the 84th floor of Taipei 101 to have my bandages taken off. They'd covered my eye for a whole day, and the moment they came off, I looked at the clinic's deep blue carpet and the golden wallpaper along the edges, and I just stood there, frozen. So that's what blue looks like. So blue has this many layers. My head had always known that, but it was the first time I'd actually seen it with my own eyes.

I grew up with a single mom and never met my dad. People at church sometimes ask whether I miss him, and I always tell them I never "had" a dad, so I don't know what having one feels like. Good or bad, strict or gentle, I never experienced any of it. When you've never had something, it's actually hard to know what it feels like to be missing it.

Cataracts are pretty much the same. I'd never seen colors correctly, so before the surgery, I had no idea I wasn't seeing clearly. People who can't see clearly don't feel like they can't see clearly. They just assume that's how the world looks.

## Home, and my old videos

When I got home after the surgery, I opened my computer and looked at videos I'd color graded before. Watching them, I stopped. No, that's not the color I wanted.

The trouble is, some of those videos had already gone to clients, and some cinematographer friends had seen them and said, "Oh, that's Kimi's color style." Back then, watching them myself, I thought so too. Yes, that's exactly the right color. But looking back now, it was more like what I thought was right. I thought I'd chosen a style. Really, those were the only colors I could see, so those were the ones I chose.

That honestly scared me a bit. Fifteen years of making video. How many times had I handed something over that was only "what I thought was right"?

## Brands seem to do this a lot too

Later I talked it over with friends like Rick and Wei-Huan, and it turns out it's not just me. Between us we've worked with hundreds of brands, and one of the most common situations is that the brand doesn't know why it's doing this either. They say, "Okay, make me a video." I ask who it's for and what they want it to achieve, and they say, "You know that one so-and-so made that went viral? I want one like that."

At that point, even if you got Ang Lee to shoot it, he'd still start by asking what you want to make. If you say you don't know, you just saw someone else do it, there's nothing Ang Lee can do, and there's even less Rick and I can do. What we have is craft. We can make a brand look beautiful and polished. But what you want to become is something you have to decide yourself.

What I think is scarier is not knowing what you don't know. You assume you're already doing it right. You make 100 videos, each one gets 500 views, and you keep doing it the same way. There's a line often credited to Einstein (if you check, he probably never said it) that goes something like: doing the same thing and expecting a different result is insanity. When I read that, well, I felt a little guilty.

I don't have the full answer yet either. I'm 41, still on the way. The reminder I got the day of the surgery was actually small: if you're willing to admit you might not be seeing clearly, you still have a chance to refocus. But if you keep holding on to "what I thought was right," you don't even get the chance.

The wall on the fourth floor of the exhibition had the things I care about written on it: light, family, myself, the things I believe. That's the version I can see right now, and I'll probably adjust it again later. The next time someone shows me their video, I'll probably start by asking: "Is this what you want, or what you think you want?"

And you? Is there something lately that you've been sure was right, that might also be worth taking out and refocusing?
