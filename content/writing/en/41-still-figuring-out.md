About every six months I do a bit of soul-searching. I pour myself a glass of water, sit down, and ask myself, very seriously, "Who am I actually supposed to become in this life?" And then my mind goes blank. Not the kind of blank where you think for a while and don't reach a conclusion. The kind where there's no direction at all. It's kind of funny when you think about it. I'm 41, I've been making video for 15 years, I've shot 30-something music videos, I've worked with Audi, LVMH and Dior, and if you ask me to say clearly what kind of person I want to become, I still can't tell you.

Every time I come up empty, the first voice that pops up in my head is incredibly familiar. "You're 41 and you're still thinking about this?" "Other people had successful startups by thirty." "All these years in the business and you don't even know what you want to do?" My guess is you've heard that voice too.

After going through this many times, what I've started to care about more is that scolding voice. Not having it figured out at 41 seems, honestly, kind of okay. But not having it figured out at 41 and scolding yourself for it every day? That's the part that's actually sad.

## People in their 60s are still changing lanes

I know some people in their 60s who are still changing jobs every few years, learning new things, changing direction. They've never really "figured it out" either. On the flip side, I know people who were completely sure of themselves at 25 and charged straight ahead. Some of them really did become very successful, but quite a few hit 30 or 35 and suddenly realized that what they'd thought was clear wasn't so clear after all, and started over.

So nobody seems to have made a rule that you have to figure it out by a certain age. Where did we even pick up the idea that you're supposed to plan your life out early? I'm honestly not sure. Maybe from reading too many self-help books...

## I've been deadlifting lately

When we were kids, grown-ups loved saying the process matters more than the result. We'd all roll our eyes and think it was just something people say to make you feel better. But as I've gotten older, I've slowly started to think, huh, that one might actually be true.

I've been doing weight training lately, and I can deadlift 95 kilos (about 210 pounds) now. But honestly, 95 isn't that impressive a number. If I stopped training one day and the muscle went away, six months later I'd probably be down to 60. What's more interesting to me is the fact that I'm training at all right now.

So these days I don't push myself so hard to have it all figured out. "I don't know" will probably be my state for a long time, so fine, I'll think about it every six months, and if nothing comes, I'll keep doing what's in front of me. The one thing I do is, after every project, I ask myself a few questions. Did I enjoy this? Which part did I feel the most? Why am I doing this? I don't rush to a conclusion. I just write it down. Keep that up for three years, and I should slowly start to see some patterns, right?

There's one more small change: I've swapped "who do I want to be" for "what do I want to do next." Identity is really hard to pin down. Director, entrepreneur, writer: each one seems right, and also not quite right. But things to do are much more concrete. Getting Joshua Band's songs heard by more people, helping brand owners learn to tell their own stories, making sure the people behind the scenes in the video industry get seen. I've been doing these things for a long, long time, and I still want to keep doing them.

If you happen to be stuck on this question too, can we agree to stop scolding ourselves for now? Scolding doesn't make anything clearer. Just keep doing what's in your hands. A lot of the time, you only start to understand it while you're doing it.

I'm 41 and still on my way. At 72 I probably will be too. Okay, for today, I'll just finish this glass of water.
