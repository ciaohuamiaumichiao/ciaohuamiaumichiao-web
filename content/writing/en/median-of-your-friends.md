You've probably heard this one: your income is roughly the median of your five closest friends. I always thought it was something artsy types said to insult people, and I let it go in one ear and out the other. But after watching people for a long time, I think there's actually something to it. It's not that you literally become your friends' average. It's more that the group's habits, the way they spend money, the rhythm of their lives, slowly pull you up or drag you down. Hang out with people who always eat at street stalls and you'll get used to street stalls. Hang out with people who pick a new restaurant every week and you'll slowly start picking too. If everyone around you buys a home in their early thirties, you'll probably start going to open houses yourself.

That used to happen only in real life. There's only so many people you can see, only so many who'll ask you to dinner. Social media came along and blew the whole thing up.

## We didn't even do anything

One day I watched two videos about brain fog and dementia. The next day my wife sent me a health article she'd just read. The day after that, my phone popped up three more related posts. The two of us hadn't done anything in real life, but the algorithm had already quietly filed us both under "interested in health content." Facebook, Instagram and TikTok are all doing the same thing: linking you with the people you interact with most into one information pool. What you see, what gets pushed to you, and who you get pushed to all depend on that pool.

## Now apply it to building a brand

If you're always liking and commenting on a few fitness influencers, the algorithm locks you in the same room with them, and your content mostly gets shown to people interested in fitness. Follow only artsy types, and you get shown to artsy types. Sounds obvious, right? But it gets pretty interesting in practice, because who you get seen by depends on more than your headlines and SEO. A big part of it is who you interact with day to day.

The people I follow fall roughly into three groups: fellow filmmakers and creatives, brand owners and marketers, and writers and people who write about philosophy. That last group is in charge of feeding my brain. I spend about 20 minutes a day on their stuff, and I only comment when I really have something to say. Over time, the algorithm started showing me to people like them. It's not a big audience, but it's a focused one. For me, 100 people who really care about video and brands are far more useful than 10,000 people passing by.

So tonight, how about spending 10 minutes trying this? Open Instagram, look at the list of people you follow, then look at whose stories you've been interacting with lately, and ask yourself: do I want to become like them? If I keep getting pushed to this group for the next five years, am I okay with that? If you're not so sure, then move the list around a bit. Add a few people you want to learn from, unfollow a few you no longer feel anything for, and interact a little more with the group you want to get closer to.

In real life we're all a bit picky about who we make friends with. Online, it seems like we can be a little picky too.
