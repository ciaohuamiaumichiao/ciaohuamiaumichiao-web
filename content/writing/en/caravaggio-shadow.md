## Usually, you'll find an introduction like this on Wikipedia

A few hundred words that sum up your whole sprawling life, short and sharp.

> Caravaggio's life was intoxicating, dangerous and full of mystery. In 1600 he suddenly appeared on the Roman art scene, and although he didn't care much about making a living, he was never short of commissions or patrons. The earliest account of him dates from 1604 and describes how he had lived about three years before: "after a fortnight's work he will swagger about for a month or two, a servant following him, from one ball-court to the next, always ready to argue or fight, so that he is most awkward to be around." In 1606 he killed a young man in a brawl, a price was put on his head, and he fled Rome. In 1608 he was caught up in another fight in Malta, and in 1609, in Naples, yet another, this time possibly an attempt on his life by an unknown enemy. The following year, after a career as a painter of little more than ten years, Caravaggio died. ([Wikipedia](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-tw/%E5%8D%A1%E6%8B%89%E7%93%A6%E4%B9%94), Chinese edition)

But usually, your life is more than those few hundred words. It's painting after painting, as beautiful and as deep as they come. Or maybe a passing thought of yours, or everything you worked so hard on, has already become the butterfly flapping its wings that shapes the course of civilization.

## This is the kind of man Caravaggio was

From his rise to the mystery of his fall, he's enigmatic and leaves plenty of blank space for the imagination. Every event in his lifetime deserves to be guessed at and puzzled over, by anyone, from any angle.

In the film *Caravaggio's Shadow*, a dashing Louis Garrel plays a secret agent in black who sits outside the Church's normal chain of command. Carrying the Church's files, he visits each of the key people in them who crossed paths with Caravaggio. Through his reading of those files (or ours, as the audience), and the Caravaggio described by each of these people, we spend two hours getting to know Caravaggio's fascinating… second half of life. (Or should that be lower half?!)

## How the film handles light and shadow

It's a complete tribute to the beauty of Caravaggio's paintings: real, and almost hard to believe. You start to wonder whether you've actually stepped into the scene. Around 1600, the average light level was surely less than a tenth of what we have today. The dim, held-back light, the lower classes covered in dust, the food of the poor, the dirty clothes and grimy faces, even the colors the upper classes wore: all of it convinces you this is how things really looked. The muted palette also pulls a thread of faith out of Caravaggio himself, and if you've studied Christian theology, you'll see the seeds of the Enlightenment.

![Still from Caravaggio's Shadow, Caravaggio talking with a woman](/writing/vocus/caravaggio-shadow/02.jpg)

## "Your work sows doubt, when they (ordinary people) should simply believe."

Caravaggio reinvented the job of painting for the Church. It wasn't just work that kept him fed. Even more, the Church was his stage. In painting after holy painting, he used ordinary people as models: prostitutes, vagrants, beggars, the people at the very bottom of society taken in at the church of Santa Maria in Vallicella. They were all drawn from his own life, and they became the faces of the saints in his paintings.

He painted the Virgin Mary, Saint Luke and Saint Peter, imagining what the disciples who followed Jesus might really have looked like. With light close to the real thing, and bodies and faces left unretouched, he got people thinking again about the deeper meaning of the Church and its teaching, and tried to hand the right to interpret that teaching back from the Church itself to ordinary people. Of course, that's also what touched the Church's nerve, and why he went down in art history but couldn't stay long in his own time.

He often said that none of the academy-trained court painters understood his work. But to me, maybe they really did understand it. They understood, and that's why they wanted to save Caravaggio's life in *this* world. Caravaggio himself was looking toward a reward in *eternity*, and he didn't back down one bit.

## What to watch for

Besides Caravaggio's paintings, and the whole film's astonishing use of his visual style, the mood of the love scenes is, of course, very close to the paintings too. The dialogue is beautiful and it sounds wonderful! Best enjoyed by viewers who are suitably mature in body and mind.
