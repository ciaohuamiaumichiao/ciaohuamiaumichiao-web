[Giuseppe Tornatore](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9C%B1%E5%A1%9E%E4%BD%A9%C2%B7%E6%89%98%E7%B4%8D%E5%A4%9A%E9%9B%B7) was 38 when he finished the film that moved the whole world, [*Cinema Paradiso*](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%A4%A9%E5%A0%82%E7%94%B5%E5%BD%B1%E9%99%A2) (*Nuovo Cinema Paradiso*). From then on he chased his film dreams as someone already in the hall of fame. Then, as he was getting ready to shoot [*The Legend of 1900*](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%B5%B7%E4%B8%8A%E9%8B%BC%E7%90%B4%E5%B8%AB), he crossed paths with [Ennio Morricone](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%81%A9%E5%B0%BC%E5%A5%A5%C2%B7%E8%8E%AB%E9%87%8C%E7%A7%91%E5%86%85). That started their collaboration and gave us the classic *The Legend of 1900*. At that point Ennio had only scored 350 of the 500 films he would score in his life.

![Banner poster for Ennio, with Ennio conducting in a study piled with sheet music](/writing/vocus/ennio-the-maestro/01.jpg)

Last night a director I look up to invited me at the last minute to a preview screening. For me that kind of chance comes along maybe once in a lifetime, so of course I went. And the film turned out to be the documentary about Ennio Morricone (released in Taiwan as 配樂大師顏尼歐, and in some markets as *Ennio: The Maestro*). I was full of anticipation for a film history told through the life of the man who created the film composer's job and gave it its highest meaning. Then I saw the director's name, and the only thing I thought was: my bladder had better be strong tonight.

## A world film history, told through film music

The film is presented by Wong Kar-wai and directed by Giuseppe Tornatore, and he managed to get a full interview with Ennio himself (who passed away in 2020...). You hear from one legendary director after another about how Ennio influenced and inspired them, and you start to realize every one of them was, in a way, trained by Ennio: how they understood film, how they thought about music, their own musical tastes, what they knew about Bach, and so on. And you find out that some of these great directors didn't like the music Ennio had carefully crafted, and loved the throwaway stuff he gave them instead. (The whole room cracked up.)

## A legendary trilogy

His elementary school classmate [Sergio Leone](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%A1%9E%E5%90%89%E6%AD%90%C2%B7%E6%9D%8E%E6%98%82%E5%B0%BC) made the Italian westerns that changed the world: [*A Fistful of Dollars*](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%8D%92%E9%87%8E%E5%A4%A7%E9%8F%A2%E5%AE%A2), [*For a Few Dollars More*](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%BB%83%E6%98%8F%E9%9B%99%E9%8F%A2%E5%AE%A2) and [*The Good, the Bad and the Ugly*](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%BB%83%E9%87%91%E4%B8%89%E9%8F%A2%E5%AE%A2). That cheeky whistled melody in them came from Ennio, a new direction born out of his love of experimenting.

Based on how the film presents him, I divided his life into three acts.

### Setting out in pure music, full of confidence

When he finished at the conservatory, he entered the arranging course as a trumpet player and learned everything he could from his mentor, and he was already picking up a lot of outside gigs and arranging work on the side. I wouldn't say he was dazzling yet, but his mentor clearly saw how hard he worked, and Ennio really did do every job in front of him as well as it could be done. Early in his career he worked at RCA, and his arrangements produced many of the hit songs of that time. At the height of that success he started scoring films, and the documentary shows how, coming from a pure-art background, film scoring became a hurdle in his life that he had to face and break through.

### Film scoring, creativity let loose

Starting from that pure-art background, and adding sharp powers of observation and a refusal to follow the rules, he brought the techniques of classical music (counterpoint, fugue and so on) into film scores. He repeated the success he'd had with records, and opened up a whole new view of what film music could be. The more he played, the further he went. At practically every stage of his life he invented a new method or technique for scoring. As recording technology raced ahead, he quickly mastered things like multitrack recording, and a lot of great directors were left with their mouths hanging open watching him swap music around on the spot.

His creativity, his high success rate and his high "booking rate" (basically, everyone wanted him) eventually brought criticism of his famously high output: how could anyone write that fast and that much?

None of that seemed to bother him. All we know is that he kept looking forward to the day he could put down film scoring and just be himself.

### Overturning himself

So, from one classic to the next, his baton drew the borders of film music, and stroke by stroke he created possibilities nobody had imagined! For newer film fans, the greatest film composer of our time might be [Hans Zimmer](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-tw/%E6%BC%A2%E6%96%AF%C2%B7%E5%AD%A3%E9%BB%98). But in his interview in the documentary, Hans talks about the Ennio he knew exactly the way you'd talk about your idol.

After he received the Academy's honorary lifetime award, everyone assumed he was heading into retirement. Instead he went back to a genre he hadn't scored in 40 years, the western, and the wish he made for himself was to overturn his old self.

That's how we got Quentin Tarantino's *The Hateful Eight*, scored by Ennio, and the film that finally won him the Academy Award for Best Original Score. I heard that at the preview screening over at the Vieshow theater, some people burst into tears and some stood up and cheered!

## A film score is a blank page

Near the end, Ennio talks about how it all starts with just an idea and a blank sheet of paper, with no idea at all what lies ahead, but time keeps moving forward, and "you have to keep writing."

That's when I felt the calling he carried on his shoulders. He never got lazy and set it down, and he never got smug about it. He took the person he loved with him, carried his calling, and kept working the ground without letting up. Fame and money weren't the goal. Being able to live inside his own calling was what satisfied him most.

After the film, I looked at his deep smile and the slightly kind look in his eyes, and it was as if he was telling me: "Kid, take your blank page and write what you need to write!" Oh, and there was also the bladder training Giuseppe Tornatore gave me.

When the credits started rolling, I was glad it was a documentary, so they wouldn't be too long, and soon enough the music rights section came up. But I'd forgotten this was a documentary about a great film composer. The music rights section went on so long that I really couldn't get up. Add Giuseppe Tornatore's bladder training on top, and I was truly grateful that Eslite Art House has the shortest path to the restroom of any cinema in Taiwan. I went straight there without wasting a single second.

I really recommend it. It opened in Taiwan on March 17, 2023, at art houses and multiplexes from Taipei to Kaohsiung. Thank you to Wong Kar-wai for presenting this film, and to [Swallow Wings Films](https://www.facebook.com/swfilms) for bringing it to Taiwan with so much care!
