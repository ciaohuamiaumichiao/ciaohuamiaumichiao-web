Last year I subscribed on [vocus](https://www.facebook.com/vocus.cc/) to "The Life and Death of the Videotape" (錄影帶生與死), a column by [Yeh Lang: Dr. Strangenote](https://www.facebook.com/Dr.Strangenote) that I waited for impatiently every month. He announced that the writing project would be published by [ThinKingDom](https://www.facebook.com/thinkingdom), with the title reworked into *Once Upon a Time, There Was a Video Store* (從前，有個錄影帶店).

![Key visual for the From Cinema to Netflix talk, with Yeh Lang in conversation with Wang Shih](/writing/vocus/cinema-to-netflix/01.jpg)

I showed up right at the start time as promised, but it was exactly as the organizers had warned: limited space, get there early. The audience had already filled every seat, so I stood at the back listening to the two masters, Yeh Lang and Wang Shih, in conversation. When I lined up to collect my copy and saw the title, for a second I couldn't place it. Had I bought "The Life and Death of the Videotape," or *Once Upon a Time, There Was a Video Store*, or was Quentin Tarantino about to blow me up somewhere with a pitch-black (violent) joke? But honestly, the new title is spot on. It reaches the older film nerds and also catches the younger generation who have never touched a videotape. I was one of the few people in the room who raised a hand to say we still have tapes at home, but I really am a long, long way from videotapes now. So, does that make me a little younger?

![The talk at Bleak Bird Books in Huashan, two speakers in conversation](/writing/vocus/cinema-to-netflix/02.jpg)

The great Yeh Lang started with the marketing behind *To Leslie*, then followed the thread into how Oscar campaign season works, and recommended the book *Oscar Wars* for all of us to read along. The great champion of campaign season was also one of the most powerful producers in Hollywood, Harvey Weinstein, who of course ended up at the very center of the [#metoo](https://www.facebook.com/hashtag/metoo) movement.

Looking at his record now, it's colored by everything that came out during #MeToo. But Yeh Lang reminded us to turn the clock back to before the sexual assault allegations broke, and look at how hard he pushed the films he was releasing to Oscar voters: sending out videotape copies, calling them over and over, and other fairly aggressive tactics. Even so, among the many directors who had worked with him, sure enough, not one really spoke up for him.

In the old days, film marketing might go through outlets like the Hollywood Reporter. Later came the gala parties, and when word got around that some voters might be away on vacation, Oscar campaigning spread from Los Angeles to major cities around the world. Everything the studios did was aimed at getting voters to remember their film fondly.

For Harvey Weinstein, though, there was no such thing as a good or bad impression. He knew how to use every kind of marketing to get his films seen, especially by landing in the news, which was basically a huge, free opportunity for exposure. Besides opening up new channels for exposure, he had a feel for the mood of public opinion. He broke through many of the taboos of the time, tied sex to art-house film, and played the ratings system: attacking the censors on one hand while cooking up marketing around sex and violence on the other.

I have to say this whole "prologue" about campaign season had me completely spellbound. And there was one thing in it I'd never noticed: a key piece of campaign season was the videotape.

In 1989, *Sex, Lies, and Videotape* kicked off a wave of independent film. Its marketing was run by Miramax, which is to say Harvey Weinstein, and it caught the attention of an entire generation. Independent film put up very satisfying box office numbers. And because videotape had arrived, it completely broke down people's distance from the equipment. Ordinary people could record images onto tape, even without film school, and the road to making things got much wider. I benefited from this myself, of course. It's what let me make all kinds of video projects at relatively low cost!

Videotape also led to something surprising, a small detail I'd never thought about. Remember how every voter was getting videotape copies mailed to them by the studios? What does that mean? It means that quite possibly, from the year those tapes started arriving at voters' homes, the way Oscar films were judged dropped from the big screen to videotape. In other words, the Best Pictures we've seen since then were shaped by generous marketing budgets, and underneath that, they weren't really theater films. They were videotape films!

The effect, coming from the studio side, could reach into the filmmaking itself. Actors' performances might move from the original medium shot to close-ups for the key moments, to suit videotape's low resolution and small picture size. So we got more and more close-ups, and fewer and fewer *Saving Private Ryan*s.

On top of that, as the format evolved from videotape to streaming, it even changed the culture of watching films. With tape rentals, people could rewatch as often as they liked, so fans kept finding more of the hidden content and easter eggs they were hoping to find. Little by little, classic film criticism faded, and fan-style sharing took its place. Fan communities formed, independent creators grew, and all of that also shaped which films studios picked and how creators think (Marvel everywhere, heh).

![Yeh Lang's signature on the title page of Once Upon a Time, There Was a Video Store](/writing/vocus/cinema-to-netflix/03.jpg)

There were lots more inside stories that made me smile, all the history between Netflix and Blockbuster and so on... I'm not trying to sell you the talk, really. It's all in Yeh Lang's book, and you truly, truly shouldn't miss it! If you're studying film, or just interested in film, it's very hard to find a book like this that takes in the whole sweep of history, given that Chinese-language film books are usually dominated by aesthetics-first criticism. If I ever go back to teaching, I'll definitely put this book on the required reading list.

So I think you should get yourself a copy of *Once Upon a Time, There Was a Video Store* too.
