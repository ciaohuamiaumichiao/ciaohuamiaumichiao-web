A few days ago Pei-Yu and I went on our good friend Jamie's show. On the show she ended up as "Aroha," because the last time she tried to listen in she typed a quick hello and it came out as Aroha, so this time she walked in already named Aroha. I wasn't much better off. My handle wouldn't fit, so I became "Kimi Meow." Same thing happens to me in Pokémon. So there we were, one Aroha and one Kimi Meow, talking about 348 kilometers for an entire evening.

First, how it started. House of Dream turned 20 this year. Most people would think of an exhibition or a show, or bringing back the kids they've served for a big event. But as Pei-Yu's team kept talking with the founder, Pastor Wen-Hua, he said: I want to walk to Taitung. Pei-Yu says the first time she heard it, the only thing in her head was: seriously? After many, many long conversations with him, she finally understood what he meant. Taipei to Taitung is an hour by plane, four or five hours by train. But when you walk alongside a kid who wants to turn their life around, you might spend a year or two and they move forward a little. Another two or three years and they take a few more steps. Education is a road you can't drive fast on. So he wanted to walk it.

How did I get involved? For me it was simple. It was Pei-Yu's project, and she asked, "Want to shoot a documentary?" I said sure, let's do it. Of course I'm happy when I hear "documentary." Then I asked, what documentary? She said: walking.

## All my homework, and none of it happened

I'm the director, so I already had a documentary in my head. It needed a bit of tragic grandeur, you know? One man struggling down the road for these kids. Or realizing halfway that he overestimated himself and can't make it that far, but has to make it that far, and almost gives up. We knew the film would screen at the November 13 event, so we worked backwards from that date: how much time, what structure, how much footage. We really did design some content, guessing what he might be feeling at certain points so we could capture it.

Then after every conversation with the pastor, I realized everything I'd imagined was the exact opposite of what he wanted. None of the things we'd designed happened, and even the tone got overturned again at every meeting. On the show, the host said she'd been following House of Dream and the pastor on Instagram the whole time, and it looked like a very cheerful walk: ice cream one day, the pastor's wife visiting the next. Where's the tragic part? Yes. Exactly. That's how it really was.

The funniest part is that out of the whole project, the pastor was the only person who actually walked every step. At one point we offered him a ride for a stretch. He was very clear: he would walk it on his own two feet, one step at a time.

## A toilet every 40 minutes

Jamie asked what we remembered most from the 16 days. Pei-Yu thought about it and said: finding toilets. We both agreed that was worth talking about, and that it was probably the main topic of the night.

I'd scouted the whole route by car beforehand, covering four or five days of walking in a day. No problem. But in a car you don't learn how often a walker needs a bathroom. Once we were actually walking, it was about every 40 minutes to an hour. It's hot, you keep drinking water, and even though most of it probably evaporates in the sun, you still need to go. In Yilan we didn't notice at all, because the head of House of Dream's Yilan center walked the pastor around to visit people, like he'd found a treasure and wanted to show everyone: look, Brother Wen-Hua is here! Every so often we'd end up sitting in someone's living room, so toilets were never an issue. After Yilan, I took over leading the group, a few more women joined us, and we hit a challenge we'd never met while scouting.

Some small train stations on the east coast only get three or four trains a day, so they just lock the toilets. We'd planned to walk an hour and rest there, we'd arrive, locked, and we'd drive ahead to find the next one. Once we'd even arranged a time in advance and the person said sure, sure, you're welcome. When the group arrived, the person in charge had gone home for the day, and the toilet had gone home with them. So our daily routine became: where's the toilet 10 km out, 5 km out? We'd drive ahead to check, report back to the staff member in charge of safety, strike up conversations with a lot of strangers, and even knock on school doors. By the end we were seriously discussing whether to start a toilet foundation. Pei-Yu still has the list of toilet locations, and the host immediately said she'd borrow it if she ever does a long walk.

## The day from Ruifang to Fulong

If I had to pick the hardest stretch, it was probably day three, from Ruifang to Fulong. In the old days, people heading to Yilan crossed the mountains on the Caoling or Jinzibei historic trails. Now you can go through the Sandiaoling Tunnel or the Old Caoling Tunnel. We had to decide which way the group would go, so Pei-Yu and I split up to scout. I dropped her at the start of the Caoling Trail and drove on to Jinzibei. Mine was shorter, so after I finished the climb I drove back to pick her up. It had just rained for days and the trail was slippery. If you ever see our behind-the-scenes footage, you'll see the two of us constantly saying encouraging things to ourselves into the camera.

After the climb we both knew: in good weather, sure, it's a fun hike. But with rain, and not knowing how often each staff member hikes, crossing the mountain was too big a risk for everyone's stamina and safety. We picked the easiest option, the tunnel, and the price was walking nearly 30 km that day, the second-longest day of the whole trip. The longest was the last day, walking into Taitung.

## We didn't treat it like a job

Jamie asked what we went in with: was this a job, or a calling? I said I didn't really feel either. It was a job, of course, but I noticed that everyone during those 16 days had a kind of looseness. People were just doing things as their natural selves. We'd also really hoped to live as the same group of people as our client, breathing the same air, eating together, walking together, being alive in the same time and place. My own goal was to get everyone out there safely and bring everyone back safely, and along the way we accidentally shot some video, and then that video accidentally needed to be released.

Pei-Yu said something that really stuck with me. What reassured her most on this trip was realizing that everyone, besides getting their tasks done, was thinking about how to take care of each other. Someone handled everyone's meals, Yi-Ru on the support van kept an eye on everyone's physical condition every day, and staff back in Taipei were cutting social videos. A lot of people did far more than you'd expect. We've known each other a long time, and I often think of us as a very strong net. We don't necessarily need the client to give us five stars, but along the way, we had five-star time together. Something like that.

The funniest moment was when the host asked if there was a moment we suddenly saw each other in a new light. We both went silent, did rock-paper-scissors, thought for a long time, and then admitted at the same time that we're half-amnesiac about a lot of the details from those 16 days. We just remember being together the whole time and feeling content. What we actually talked about, we've honestly forgotten. The host said we were probably too relaxed and had too many golden memory orbs.

One thing I do remember clearly, though. As a documentary director, my move is asking sharp questions, trying to break down someone's defenses so they drop whatever they're pretending to be. I was digging at the pastor the whole way, and I found out he's simply the same person inside and out. Who he is on camera is who he is. Of course, maybe I just hadn't used all my moves yet. Pei-Yu said she saw an everyday version of the pastor: he'd say outright that he missed his wife, and he insisted on getting ice cream. By my estimate, he had ice cream on seven or eight out of every ten days, to the point where the people closest to him started saying, I'm not going for ice cream with you anymore.

## Only to Meet You

Why not walk all the way around Taiwan, just Taipei to Taitung? Because Hualien and Taitung are where House of Dream wants to slowly grow next. Along the way, pastors from Yilan really did show up, people from Hualien showed up, and people who've been serving in Taitung for years showed up. That's why the film is called *Only to Meet You* (只為遇見你). I walked so much, and so far, really just to see you once.

The film was still in the edit when we recorded. We said on the show that if the answer at the next screening is yes, this would probably be a near-perfect collaboration for me. It premiered on the evening of November 13 at House of Dream's Ximen venue in Taipei.

Finally, thank you to Jamie for having us, to Pei-Yu, and to Yi-Ru, who got pulled onto the show at the last minute. Happy 20th birthday, House of Dream.
