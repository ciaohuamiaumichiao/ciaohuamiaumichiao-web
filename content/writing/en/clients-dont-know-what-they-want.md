The client on the other end of the phone says, "I want to do a video. Shoot one for me." I ask where it's going, who it's for, and what he wants it to do. He says, "You know, like what's-his-name. His thing went viral. I want one of those."

Every time I hear that I want to laugh, but once I'm done laughing, what I actually do is sigh. Fifteen years in, I get some version of this call about once a month, so regularly that I could almost lip-sync along.

I've talked about this with Rick and a few other director friends. More than eight out of ten brands we run into are stuck in the same place: they don't know why they're doing this. Other people have Instagram, so I need Instagram. Other people make Reels, so I need Reels. But ask them who the audience is, how they want to be remembered, or who they want to get paid by, and the line goes quiet. This honestly has very little to do with how good anyone's craft is. You could hire Ang Lee and he'd still ask you first what you want to make. If you say "I saw other people doing it," Ang Lee can't help you either, and I definitely can't. What we know is craft. Craft can make things look beautiful, but it can't decide for you who you want to be.

## Well, you can't really blame the client

I used to think clients were just being lazy. Later I realized that's not quite fair. School teaches you Excel, teaches you to make a deck, teaches you your profession, but nobody ever teaches you to sit down and really think about what your brand is actually for. And meanwhile everyone outside keeps telling you to just start: open the account, shoot something, run ads, figure it out once you're moving. You're moving, sure, but with no direction, so every time you start from zero. A hundred videos and five hundred posts later, the brand still hasn't grown into anything. Thinking about it, that sounds exhausting.

## So what are my three questions?

First: who do you want to be seen by? "As many people as possible" doesn't count. It has to be a specific group. Maybe 35-year-old designer moms, maybe young people just out of school who want to start a business, maybe small business owners with good taste in visuals.

Second: once they've seen you, how do you want them to remember you? Cheap, fast and simple? Or high quality, a little slower, but worth it? Those two will be completely different films.

Third: who do you want to get paid by? Sounds a little mercenary, right? But this one really matters. If you're serving people with small budgets, you need volume and low prices. If you're serving wealthy people, it has to feel high quality, without being so cool and distant that nobody dares come near.

These days, before I take a job, I spend 30 to 60 minutes going through these three questions properly with the client. It's not exactly a free consultation. It's more like checking whether we have the basis to work together. The ones who are willing to sit down and think it through with me usually go smoothly from there. As for the ones who don't even want to think about it, I'll turn them down, or split it into two stages: brand consulting first, then the shoot.

So if you're planning to hire someone to make a video, why not try answering these three questions yourself first? It's really okay if you can't. Just pause for a while, and call a director once it's a bit clearer. By then, everyone will have a much easier time getting the work done.
