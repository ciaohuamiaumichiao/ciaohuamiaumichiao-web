## Evil is spreading through the multiverse, and the world is about to end

Evelyn (Michelle Yeoh), a 55-year-old Chinese American immigrant, is watching her family and her business fall apart at the same time. On the day of her elderly father's (James Hong) big birthday, she has to stop her daughter (Stephanie Hsu) from blowing up and coming out, clean up after her useless husband (Ke Huy Quan), and go to the tax office to explain some not-so-great tax problems to a stiff auditor (Jamie Lee Curtis). The moment they step into the building's elevator, her spineless husband suddenly starts acting like he's in *The Matrix*, claiming he's a version of himself from another universe, and that Evelyn is one of millions of versions of her across the universes. Before she can make sense of it, evil is spreading through the multiverse and the world is about to end, and only Evelyn, good for nothing in this damn universe, can save it...

## Life is often just this frustrating

For a "film person" like me, who has only dipped a toe into the film industry, my love of movies has always been something on the level of faith. A good friend of mine went to study in the US, and I follow his American life every day. Our taste in films was never quite the same, but news sent back from the front lines of cinema like that is always worth special attention.
I'd been fired up by the teaser for this one very early on. I really struggle with a pure American accent, but this very Asian-flavored English pulled me right in. For once, my broken English got me through the teaser and the trailer without subtitles.
Then one day I saw my friend give this film a perfect score in his Instagram story, which meant I had to clench my fists and charge straight out to buy a ticket. Unfortunately, I ran into moving house, work, home quarantine and my kid's school shutting down. When school finally reopened for two days, I rushed out to catch this and *Top Gun: Maverick*. Good thing I did, because school is shut again now. Life is often just this frustrating.

I don't think the frustration of the opening needs much explaining. Evelyn's busyness and helplessness are so overwhelming that even those of us sitting in the theater start to get restless. The high point of the first stretch has to be the scene where Evelyn explains to Gong Gong who her daughter's "friend" is. It piles on traditional, conservative Chinese thinking and the habit of putting harmony before everything else. For Evelyn and Joy, this is the first emotional peak, and I think however detached you are, after this scene you can't help fully empathizing with the characters.

Once they get to the tax office, it's time to explain how the multiverse works this time around. It isn't a point where time and space intersect, like Makoto Shinkai's *Your Name*, and it isn't time running out of sync, like Christopher Nolan's *Tenet*.

What I love most is that on top of the idea of a parallel universe, this film adds the multiverse, and gives a plain, easy-to-follow explanation and breakdown of the universe's time and space on another level. And the whole plot runs on a very precise use of the core idea of the [three-act structure](https://edumovie-tfai.org.tw/activities_info.php?id=168), so the title alone tells you everything the film is going to play out.

## S1: Everything

Usually, when every event shows up tangled together at once, the director telling the story can easily get flustered with the timeline. But the Daniels calmly sort through Evelyn's situation, step by step. They lay out the outer, rational layer (her father's birthday, her daughter's girlfriend, the shop's taxes), then the problems she might face on the inner rational layer (her relationship with Waymond, her place with her daughter, how she lives with herself), and gradually open up the needs in her heart (where family and her partner stand, how she holds in and lets out her feelings). Just by setting up these three events, they make the question the whole film has to answer plain for all of us to see.

## S2: Everywhere

After the curtain rises on the essay question, I love how the film uses this act's name to tell us how it plans to untangle a problem that has weighed on humans forever. It tells us with *Everywhere*:

There's the Alpha universe solution: you need to know you're someone like Neo, even if your bullet time comes out as a failure, or even if there's always someone inside you who you assumed was your Professor X, your teacher, but who turns out to be the evil mastermind.

There's the kung fu universe solution: live as the most ideal version of yourself, then look back at what the real you actually wants. When Evelyn is playing Michelle Yeoh, and Michelle Yeoh leans against the wall listening, is she Chow Mo-wan, or is she Su Li-zhen? For a moment, the me in the real universe seemed to tumble into my own Hong Kong cinema universe, and in that short stretch I'd already relived *Chungking Express*, *In the Mood for Love* and *Days of Being Wild*. Of course, in this universe you'll always find the love of your life, and in this universe that love lives forever in your heart, but is never by your side.

There's the hot dog universe solution: maybe what you need isn't a great love, or your family, but a best friend, the kind of close girlfriend traditional Chinese culture can't make sense of, and in her you find empathy, and a way to shrug off a merciless reality.

And even the universe of pure consciousness, where no animals exist at all: the rocks! (I hated how the Taiwanese subtitles translated this part.) Living at ease like a stone, that's how it should be, right? But even sitting there as immovable as a mountain, a mother's burning, unquenchable love for her child still drives the whole world to chase after a child who's suffocating from being loved.

## S3: All at Once

Who the final boss is doesn't matter anymore. The monster we think we're facing usually turns out to be something we made ourselves. If you grew up as a Chinese kid, you tend to know this suffocating kind of love. It chases you until there's nowhere left to run, and it pressures you until you can't say anything out loud. It's so intense it makes you want to leave, and then once you've left, you endlessly copy that awful experience onto whatever next generation you're able to reach.

After the events of the first act have been run through the calculations of the second, the final boss may be using some multi-layered algorithm to mess with the relationships between universes, but in the writers' hands, the third act always gives us the answer to the calculation: the result of characters' personalities piling up on each other. Unexpected, and yet it makes complete sense.

This ending could easily be used as a psychology textbook. Personally, I loved it, and for me it may also have been a kind of release in my own real universe!

I spent the whole movie waiting to find out what the multiverse is. Or have I spent my whole life waiting for that answer?

Making peace with yourself, living alongside the person you love, making peace and living alongside your kids: how many layers are there to that, and how hard is it? That's not a question math can answer. But I'll always remember Waymond's confession to Evelyn in the kung fu universe. I took the hand of the love of my life sitting next to me, and my eyes went blurry.

In the final conversation with Joy, I climbed into the bagel, and found that I'd never actually come out. A lot of the time we think we have to wait for some answer before we can take on the next task. But life, or the universe, doesn't leave room for you to keep waiting.

Your bagel will always be there. There's an Alpha universe in your life, and that universe needs you to be the savior who saves yourself. But you also need to find your Jobu Tupaki, because she's the "self" you made with your own hands.

> "Of all the places I could be, I just want to be here with you." (Evelyn)

That line isn't only said to the final boss. It's said to yourself, and it's said to the person I love. The most precious thing in every universe is time slipping away. You can't rewind it. You can only grit your teeth and keep going, and in that very limited time, our choices make us into whatever kind of "self" we become.
