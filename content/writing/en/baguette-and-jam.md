Before class that day, I went to the second floor of Breeze Nan Shan and bought a baguette, yes, from that super famous place. I'd also thought about bringing black tea, but then I figured tea at night would keep everyone up, so maybe fresh milk tea instead. When class started I asked around whether anyone was allergic to milk. Nobody. Darn it, I should have brought it! So in the end the table had the bread, a box of salted butter, and a jar of jam.

Everyone got a piece. I asked them to take a bite or two first, just clean and fresh, nothing added, and then write down what they tasted. Five words, five sentences, five colors, five actions, anything goes, in a notebook or on their phone. Some people ate too fast. I said no problem, no problem, and also, you can have a second piece.

## What you bring back is called raw material

People who shoot love talking about "takes" and "clips," this one, that one, because in editing software each file sits on the timeline as one strip. And all the stuff we bring back from a shoot is called raw material, our footage. If the footage is great and you can eat it as is, wonderful. That's what a livestream is: shoot it and use it straight away.

But most of the time, what directors, editors and planners do is take that footage and process it: season it, slice it, plate it. Sometimes we let you eat it, sometimes we deliberately don't. That's why I've always felt that making films, planning projects and running events are really, really similar to a chef sending out a dish.

So for the second round I brought out the butter and the jam. I'd always thought that jar was blackcurrant, and when I opened it, it turned out to be apricot. Ha. I'd also wanted to buy balsamic vinegar, but I didn't have a container for it, so I let it go. I asked everyone to take the rest of their bread, or grab a new piece, dip it, and write five more, and see how it differed from that first bite.

## I'm still a baguette

While they were writing, I talked about something I'd been thinking about for quite a while. When we create, when we worship, or just when we live, it feels like we're always refining the purest version of ourselves. But what we show people, what we bring before God, usually starts with finding something inside that purest part, and then deciding what to add this time, what to give God this time, what to give the audience.

So today I might be the me dipped in jam, next time the me with butter, but I'm still a baguette. I haven't changed. Dancing, painting, holding a camera, those are all spreads. Everyone came here today to eat the baguette, not the jam.

Honestly, I've been lost about this myself. When I first started leading worship on stage, I kept thinking about how the songs should be sung to be right, how I should look up there. Then one time I opened my eyes and realized nobody was looking at me at all. So who was I even performing for? For God, right? Then you can perform all kinds of versions. And by performing I don't mean faking it, because you can't fake anything in front of God anyway. I mean you can come to Him in different states. Jam today, butter next time, either one works.

What about you? If you're a baguette too, what have you been dipped in lately?
