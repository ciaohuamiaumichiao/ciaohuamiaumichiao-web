A dad came to me. His son was 11, homeschooled, studying dance, and wanted to learn to take pictures. He asked if I could teach him photography. I said no.

Because an 11-year-old who goes to learn photography will probably learn aperture and shutter speed, the rule of thirds, which lens to use for what. Once he's learned all that, he'll think he knows how to take pictures, but he still won't have touched the thing at the very center of it. So I told the dad: I don't teach photography, but I can watch movies with him.

## Two hours a week, watching Netflix

From that day on, I went to their house two hours a week. The dad paid, and my job was to watch Netflix with his son. Sweet gig, right? I honestly think so too.

I did pick the films, though. And afterwards we didn't discuss how many acts there were, where the turning points came, or what editing techniques were used. I asked him just one question: "What did you feel?"

At first all he'd say was "it was good" or "it was moving." I'd say okay, let's keep watching. We went from the start of seventh grade to now, when he's about to start ninth. Three years. His answers are really different now. He'll say a film touched some place inside him, or that the main character felt like they were talking to him, or that one line made him think of a certain stretch of his own life. Sometimes when we watch the same film a second time, what he feels is different from the first time. I think it's because he's lived through a few more things in between.

## Caramel pudding milk ice

A while ago I went with a group of good friends to Taiyi, the famous old milk shaved-ice shop in Taipei. Actually, I used to not like that place much. That day I ordered a bowl of caramel pudding milk ice, which is incredibly unhealthy, basically a bowl of sugar. But it was the best shaved ice I've ever had.

I don't think the ice itself was all that magical. It was that afternoon, being with people I love, and the slight bitterness of the caramel happening to match something in the conversation that day. I still remember that taste, and I didn't even take a picture. If one day I want to photograph that kind of feeling, I'll shoot it with that afternoon.

I once paid over NT$3,000 for a class where the teacher taught us how to look at art. He said the most important thing in photography is presence, making the viewer feel like they're right there. I agree. It's just that what I do is more like storing the feeling I had in that moment inside a photo, and then letting whoever looks at it have a little chat with that feeling. I'm not trying to change anything about you, and you don't need to walk away feeling like your life has been solved. If there's even a little bit of conversation, I'm happy.

## Why always an iPhone?

The photos on the fourth floor of my exhibition were almost all shot on an iPhone. It's not that I can't afford a camera, although, okay, my iPhone is on a subscription upgrade plan... It's that I really believe the difference isn't in the equipment. You want to take a picture because in that moment you're in a conversation with what's in front of you, and you press the shutter because you want to pass that feeling on to the next person. An iPhone can do that. So can a Hasselblad. The difference is whether the person pressing the shutter is actually inside that moment.

So when people ask me how to learn photography, I pretty much always say the same thing. Go watch films. While you watch, ask yourself what you're feeling, and remember it. Then use whatever you have on hand and try to capture that feeling.

I'm still practicing this myself. Let's do it together.
