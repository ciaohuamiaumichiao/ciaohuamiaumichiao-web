A while ago I went on Pastor Wen-Hua's show, *Chats with Brother Hua* (與華哥聊聊), the conversation show Truth Church puts out every Friday at 11 a.m. This was EP7, and I was the guest. The moment I walked into the studio, he called me Brother Hua (my nickname, 小花, has the "hua" that means flower) and I called him Brother Hua (his name, Wen-Hua, has a different "hua" that sounds exactly the same). Brother Hua, Brother Hua, Brother Hua. I'm pretty sure the staff on the side couldn't tell who was talking to whom. Also, we seem to be about equally handsome.

He opened with a question. He walks alongside a lot of young people, and they often change jobs every year or two, across all kinds of fields. But I've been doing the same thing for 20 years: video out in the world, media at church, and now teaching other people to do media. How on earth have I kept that passion going?

[▶ Watch the full episode on YouTube (Mandarin)](https://youtu.be/bW2Id1SUznQ)

## The $200 million question

Honestly, my first reaction when I hear about young people changing jobs all the time is: wow, lucky them. They get to choose. They can find out "I don't want this one" and go try another. Over these 20 years, I haven't really had that kind of courage.

But every time I've wanted to quit, I think of something an upperclassman passed down to us when I was a student: if someone gave you $200 million on the condition that you never make another film for the rest of your life, would you take it? If you would, then you can quit. It means you're not actually passionate about filmmaking, you just want to make money from it.

So I really did think about it. Two hundred million US dollars. I can't even count that high. What would I do with it? First, give it to church. Then, give some more. Then, I'd want to buy gear. I'd buy cameras... and once I get to cameras, I'm done. I'm already thinking about which script to find and where to go shoot. Yep, that's it. I can't give you some grand philosophy about these 20 years. It's just that every time I want to give up, that question pops up, and I think, it'd be kind of a shame not to do this.

Brother Hua said nobody had ever asked him that one, but the first thing he thought of was how many kids $200 million could take care of, and where it could send missionaries. He was also very honest that he's burned out twice in 20 years, and both times it took a long time to recover, so now he pays a lot of attention to his physical and mental health. Apart from that, though, he jumps out of bed excited every morning. He and his wife have even talked about it, and they figure they're probably never going to retire.

## The Three Little Pigs on the school stage

Then he asked when I first discovered I liked this. Wow, that one's hard, because for me it wasn't like opening a box and going, "wow, this is what I want." It wasn't love at first sight.

I grew up alone. I mean physically alone. My mom worked nights, so the two of us were basically on shifts. I went to school during the day while she slept, I got home and we handed over, she went to work in the middle of the night, and at six in the morning she'd call to wake me up. So I spent a lot of time with myself, with some imaginary friends in my head whose lives had probably played out in two hundred different versions in there. In junior high, my family bought a CD burner, and classmates would put in orders with me: which songs, tracks one through ten, in what order. I'd download them, burn them and sell them the discs. When Brother Hua heard that, he said, wow, a businessman already in junior high.

My first real contact with media was at Tainan Second Senior High School. I was in the recreation leadership club, and our history class happened to have a media and communication assignment. I picked radio drama, because I thought getting people to imagine things through sound alone was fun. At lunch my friends and I ran to the stage at the edge of the sports field with a tape recorder, you record a line, I record a line, and we acted out *The Three Little Pigs*. For the knock on the door we really knocked on the door, knock knock knock. Then we edited and edited and edited the sound, put a host at the front and an ending at the back, and that was our radio drama.

That's when I noticed I was having a great time playing with this stuff, and when we played it, everyone else had a great time too. Even the parts where I got stuck, which software to edit in, why the sound came out weird after burning, I enjoyed every problem I solved. Later I didn't do well on the university entrance exam, but I still got into the film track of the Radio, Television and Film department at Shih Hsin University. And the minimum admission score printed in the newspaper was exactly my score. I took that as proof. Okay, God wants me to study this, so I'll study it properly.

I think passion is more like building with LEGO. Every little sense of accomplishment is another piece you snap on, snap, snap, snap, and slowly it becomes your own castle. If you don't like it, that's fine, take it apart and put it somewhere else. Pre-production, the shoot, post, marketing: each part has its own kind of accomplishment to stack up, and it keeps getting more fun. A friend once introduced me as "Kimi, a man who can't be defined." I loved that, because I'm really scared of being called a cameraman, like I can't edit or something.

## The failure I'm proudest of

Brother Hua asked which project I'm proudest of. My usual answer is: the next one. But that day I wanted to talk about one that failed.

More than ten years ago I took on a music video for Joshua Band, for the song *點燃* ("Ignite"). We shot it on the roof of Taipei City Hall. The budget was tiny, so I made a decision: put all the money into renting lights. The shoot was so much fun, standing on the roof of City Hall looking out over all of Taipei. Then I got back and started editing, and it wasn't good. The shots were shaky, the footage going from daylight into night didn't cut together, and while the camera was great, I'd saved money on the wrong tripod and didn't have enough support rigging. After watching it myself, all I could think was, this looks like a grade schooler shot it.

But here's my point: I still really enjoyed it. I didn't feel defeated at all. It was a super interesting failure, and I learned every single thing from that round. And Brother Kuang let it pass. He didn't say "Kimi, this is bad, reshoot it," and he didn't say "it's bad, so we're not paying." That pastor's big heart gave me room to try and fail. So if I had to add one more trait to passion, it's that you're very happy to embrace your failures. Inside passion, there don't really seem to be any low points.

Brother Hua followed with something I loved. He said perfectionism puts out passion. His younger daughter says "Elsa" as "Elosa," and he doesn't correct her every time. When it's time, she'll learn it on her own.

## So should your hobby become your job?

Brother Hua said someone in the US found that only 4% of people end up working in what they studied. So should you turn your hobby into your job? Or just go all in on TSMC right now?

I was deciding whether to give good advice or just give my story. The good advice, of course, is that if you have a stable income, you can go chase your dream properly. My daughter and I were actually talking about this recently. We encouraged her to turn what she loves into her future job, and she said, no way. A dream is a dream and a job is a job, and when work gets tiring, I'll go dream for a bit. I thought that was great. A life with choices really is a blessing, because when I look back at my own, I don't think I ever had a choice. I never got the chance to stop for three months and really think about where my life was going.

But the inner me says yes, absolutely. I'm a contrarian. If you tell me to go east, I'll go east east east with you, and halfway there I'll think the west looks kind of nice, so come on, let's go check out the west. I don't want to be stuck at work for eight hours and only get to play in the other sixteen. Life is short, and I want to be playing at life during those eight working hours too. If what I'm getting paid right now is less, I'll hang in there, because whether it's more or less, I already got the joy out of it. I guess I'm a little bad at delayed gratification. I don't want to wait until I'm 50 to say I'll retire and travel the world. If I could, I'd travel the world right now, while making films, while bringing my kids, while earning money.

Brother Hua said he couldn't even see my taillights anymore. But he also said he's in the same camp.

## A government contract that isn't easy

I also talked about something happening right now. I recently landed a government contract that runs from November to next February. Lots of rules, hard to execute, complicated reviews, and not much money in it. There are really two ways to approach a job like that. One is: it's hard, so do it however, just get it past. But I thought, if I start November with the attitude of "ugh, this is so hard, let's just get it over with," then four months of my life go by like that. That's way too painful.

So I decided on the spot: no. Since I took it, let's beat it, and do it differently from how I've done things before. Make two versions for them to choose from, then throw in a third. Make the hard thing really fun, so I enjoy it and they're happy too. Then these four months won't be wasted. It'll be busy and tiring, but I'll learn a lot more.

Brother Hua said he's the same. He says he's slow to react, not a great talker, and a total introvert, so a sermon takes him 20 hours to prepare, and he reads a passage at least 30 times. But for him, that's God teaching him for 20 hours first, and then he takes one hour to share it with everyone. It's a huge joy.

## A toilet every 40 minutes

When you talk about hard things, you have to talk about the 348 km. Brother Hua spilled my secrets on the show. He said he thought planning meant looking at a map and reading stuff online, but I scouted the route four times before we set off, by car, by scooter, by bicycle and on foot, and while we were walking I'd still suddenly say, let's not take Provincial Highway 9, let's walk through that village instead.

But after scouting it four times, I still missed one thing: everyone needs to pee! We kept drinking water the whole way, so it was about once every 40 minutes to an hour, and somehow I never thought of it while scouting. Okay, new mission: start calculating where we'd be in 40 minutes and whether there was a toilet there. And if we have to detour for a toilet anyway, maybe we can take a different road and see a bit more. An extra 10 minutes of walking should be fine. Brother Wen-Hua won't notice anyway.

It often feels like a wuxia novel to me. In *The Return of the Condor Heroes*, Yang Guo is poisoned by the love flower, and the heartbreak grass that cures it grows just 100 meters away. Whatever poison you've got, the antidote is always nearby. The difficulty is right here, and the way through it is right beside you too, in your own creativity. Brother Hua summed it up for me at the end. He said some people keep chasing excellence and success and may end up crushed by it, but passion taken all the way will also carry things to excellence, and you don't get worn out. You actually enjoy it.

## Brother Wen-Hua, as he really is

At the end of the show, Brother Hua asked what I'd say if I were inviting friends to see *Only to Meet You*. I said, it's very rare to find a pastor willing to let someone follow him with a camera for 16 days. (Brother Hua jumped in right away: so you'll see me eating a lot of ice cream, held captive by desserts.)

But for me, the most precious thing is that the Brother Wen-Hua I filmed is just him. On stage and off, in front of the camera and behind it, he looks the same. He isn't playing a pastor. He's really, truly living right in front of you, with weakness and struggles, with joy, with funny moments, with his wisdom, and with that love of his that's so contagious. So you don't need to admire any technique of mine in this film. I simply showed what House of Dream looks like and what Brother Wen-Hua looks like, and by the end you'll know for yourself what it was we were walking together.

The film premiered on Friday evening, November 13, in the fifth-floor hall at 144 Zhonghua Road in Wanhua, Taipei, with free-will donations and entry by ticket. If you want the behind-the-scenes story of those 16 days, I wrote about it in "[How Does a Story Turn Into a 348 km Walk?](/en/writing/walking-348km-interview)"

Near the end of the episode, Brother Hua quoted a verse: a bruised reed he will not break, and a smoldering wick he will not snuff out. He said that even when passion and work come together, there will still be discouraging moments, so you have to keep adding wood to the fire, and also remember to cut yourself some slack. When it's time to shoot something casually, shoot it casually. When it's time for ice cream, go get ice cream. That's something I'd like to give you, and give myself too.

Thank you, Brother Hua, for having me, and thank you to the Truth Church team.
